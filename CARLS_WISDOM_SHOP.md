# Carl's Wisdom: Shop Practice

Carl's repeated themes are attention, secure workholding, practice, patience, and asking for help. Current class shop rules and his demonstration control every operation; these notes are a reading aid for preparing and remembering.

## Practice and workholding

> “make your mistakes on practice pieces first!”

— Carl Spagnoli, class email, PDF p. 14.

Make a practice mortise, tenon, taper, router cut, or finish sample before committing a table part. Use comparable stock and thickness so the trial tests the actual setup. Pencil-label parts, mating joints, and reference faces so that repeated operations do not become a guessing game. Remove marks that will be trapped or visible after assembly only after their job is done.

Carl emphasizes that tools apply forces in several directions. Visualize what will push, lift, twist, or pull the work, and arrange the vise, clamps, fences, bench dogs, stops, or supports to resist those forces. The quick-release vises and bench dogs in the shop are tools to learn, not furniture to work around. His [bench-dog link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bench_dog) is a vocabulary detour; have Carl demonstrate the actual bench.

For our slender legs, a hand gripping the part is not an adequate substitute for stable planing support. For machine work on small or narrow pieces, use the instructor-approved sled, push block, hold-down, sacrificial fence, or longer parent stock. See [Tapering the Legs](CARLS_WISDOM_TAPERING.md) for the special case of the second tapered face.

## Protection and attention

- Wear shop-approved eye protection that protects the sides as well as the front. Ordinary spectacles are not equivalent.
- Use the hearing and dust protection required for the operation. Audio entertainment is a distraction around power tools; active noise-cancelling earbuds are not a substitute for rated hearing protection.
- Secure loose sleeves, ties, scarves, hair, and other items that can catch. Maintain a balanced stance and move your feet instead of overreaching.
- Work rested and deliberately. Frustration is a reason to pause and ask for instruction, rather than add force or speed.
- Help classmates notice a risky setup gently, and ask Carl whenever confidence or understanding is missing.

Carl's old link to [CCAE's in-person safety guidelines](https://ccae.org/about/in-person-safety-guidelines) is a general institutional page, not a complete woodworking-machine rulebook. Follow the current class instructions.

## Setups and machines

Disconnect power according to shop procedure before changing blades, bits, or making adjustments that require it. Check that a hand power tool is switched off before connecting power. Clear loose metal tools and debris from the cutting area, confirm the required guards and workholding, and begin only as demonstrated. Carl repeatedly warns against starting a cutting tool with its edge already engaged in the work.

On the bandsaw, adjust the blade cover for the operation as taught; after use and shutdown, Carl asks that it be lowered to the table. On any machine, use its own approved feeding and holding method. A generic instruction to use a push stick does not replace the correct push block, sled, or other device for that tool.

The shop's SawStop has an injury-mitigation system. Its manufacturer explains:

> “When skin contacts the blade, the signal changes because the human body is conductive.”

— [SawStop, How the technology works](https://www.sawstop.com/why-sawstop/the-technology).

That describes a backup response, not permission to approach the blade or relax technique. Carl explicitly cautions against relying on it. Keep the shop's guards and other protections in place and follow the instructor's operation-specific method. His linked [New York Times discussion](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/03/30/upshot/table-saws-safety-cost.html) could not be read in this check; the manufacturer's explanation is the verified source here.

## Restore the shared shop

Carl asks us to leave tools ready for the next person: return tools and clamps to their places, coil cords, sweep and vacuum as directed, and remove chips and dust that can spoil accurate reference surfaces. Restore saw settings, router bits, guards, and shutdown states only by the shop's procedure, with the machine stopped and power isolated where required. Do not reach near a moving cutter to clean or reset it.

Tiny debris under a square or against a fence can make a carefully measured cut inaccurate. Cleanliness is part of working square and true, as well as part of sharing the room.

## Before the next class

**Parts to bring or identify:** four leg blanks, both flat boards, and representative offcuts. Carry the [current purchased-stock plan](CUTTING_DIAGRAM.md), rather than the archived drawer cutting examples.

| Prepare | Target or actual measurement | Tools and test pieces |
|---|---|---|
| Inspect stock | Actual thickness, clear width, usable length, square and defects | Tape/rule, square, straightedge, pencil |
| Review leg preparation | Finished target **1 1/8 x 1 1/8 x 26 3/4 in**, thickness x width x length | Shop-approved milling setup; comparable practice stock |
| Review base joints | Actual mortises determine tenon fit; verify 3/16-in apron setback | Mortise/tenon test pair; confirm front-corner layout with Carl |
| Prepare taper questions | Two inside faces, start 1 in below actual apron bottom, foot 5/8 in square | Labeled practice leg, approved support or jig |
| Preserve finish options | No finish selected; test on this poplar purchase | Labeled finish offcuts |

**Stopping point:** if the setup or front-corner layout is unsettled, finish measurement, labeling, and practice. Do not commit the real parts to an uncertain operation. Once the joints are made, require a complete dry fit of the base before glue: check diagonals, twist, setbacks, and top/support fit. Record actual values in the [measurement record](PROJECT_BASE.md#measurement-record).

Carl suggests waiting until a few classes have supplied some experience before buying many tools. Bring suitable tools you already own if useful. Eye protection and an accurate, readable tape are his early priorities; planes, chisels, saws, squares, clamps, and sharpening equipment can follow a better-informed conversation.
