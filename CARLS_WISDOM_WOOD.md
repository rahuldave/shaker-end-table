# Carl's Wisdom: Wood and Buying

Carl wants us to inspect the actual wood: both faces, both edges, both ends, its grain, its color, and especially its flatness. A beautiful board is useful only if the required parts fit into sound wood after milling.

## What we already own

Our 8 October record reports **two approximately 60-in poplar boards**, recalled as nominal 1x8, plus **four 1 1/2 x 1 1/2 x 30-in poplar leg blanks**. These are reported purchase sizes, not verified finished dimensions. See the [purchased-stock plan](CUTTING_DIAGRAM.md) before buying extra material.

Carl's email gives a useful sketch of a drawer-free table coming from two roughly 5-ft boards or a longer board cut into manageable lengths. It is an illustration of planning, rather than a guaranteed yield: checks, kerfs, width loss, grain selection, and the chosen joints all matter. Our current layout also reserves two top cleats and labels its rough allowances.

## Square stock and lumber labels

Carl recommends pre-milled square stock for most beginners' legs. It avoids the article's angled extraction for matching figure on four faces. He prefers a solid blank to laminating thinner pieces because the glue line may remain visible. Purchased square stock still needs inspection, acclimation, and checking for straightness and square.

A nominal construction-lumber name such as 1x8 is not a promise of measured size. But do not assume every hardwood dealer uses nominal dimensions either. Anderson & McQuaid's current price-list introduction explicitly says:

> “All dimensions listed are exact sizes, not nominal.”

It also says:

> “All prices are in lineal feet, not board feet, except for D3S items.”

— [Anderson & McQuaid, Price List](https://www.andersonmcquaid.com/price-list/), checked 8 October 2026.

**For our table:** verify the purchase receipt and measure the boards. Do not convert the recalled 1x8 name into a guaranteed usable width or thickness, and do not compare a price per linear foot directly with one per board foot.

Hood Distribution defines S2S as:

> “Surfaced (machined to a smooth finish) on two sides; edges rough.”

— [Common Lumber Terms](https://hooddistribution.com/resources/common-lumber-terms/).

That distinction explains why smooth faces can coexist with edges still needing work. Our [illustrated terminology](LUMBER_AND_WOODWORKING_TERMINOLOGY.md) covers S4S, quarter-thickness, board feet, kerfs, and reference surfaces. [Cline Lumber's terminology page](https://clinelumber.com/about/basic-lumber-terminology/) is another dealer-written explanation; its equipment limits describe Cline's mill, not the CCAE machines.

## Poplar and surface preparation

Carl treats species choice as a conversation about availability, appearance, patience, and the project. Our choice is already **poplar throughout**. The usual U.S. furniture-lumber yellow-poplar entry in Eric Meier's Wood Database notes:

> “Poplar can sometimes leave fuzzy surfaces and edges”

— [The Wood Database, Yellow poplar, Workability](https://www.wood-database.com/yellow-poplar/).

For our legs and top, that is a reason to try a sharp plane and the intended sanding sequence on an offcut, then examine it under raking light. Protect the soft stock from clamp dents. Keep representative pieces for finish tests, including the purchase's color variations. Do not assume the cherry article's oil/lacquer result predicts the appearance of our poplar.

Carl also links [Preventing Color Changes in Exotic Woods](https://www.wood-database.com/wood-articles/preventing-color-changes-in-exotic-woods/). Read it for perspective on how appearance changes over time, rather than as a finish specification for this table.

## Storage and seasonal movement

Ocooch's wood-care guide makes a practical point:

> “weight alone won’t keep a board flat or flatten an already cupped board.”

— [Ocooch Hardwoods, How to Flatten, Acclimate, and Store Thin Wood](https://ocoochhardwoods.com/care-of-wood/), “Does Weight Help?”

Its discussion of even air exposure and acclimation is useful. Its wetting-and-flattening instructions concern thin wood and are not an adopted treatment for our purchased boards. Ask Carl about storage, moisture, and milling yield before trying a corrective treatment.

The Forest Products Laboratory explains the underlying reason:

> “Wood, like many natural materials, is hygroscopic; it takes on moisture from the surrounding environment.”

— Samuel V. Glass and Samuel L. Zelinka, [Wood Handbook, Chapter 4: Moisture relations and physical properties of wood](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62243), 2021.

**For our table:** allow for seasonal width changes in the solid top, preserve the planned movement holes across its grain, and do not glue the top rigidly across the base. Ask the instructor to judge readiness for milling from the actual stock and shop conditions; there is no universal number of acclimation days in our plan.

## What to measure before cutting

Record actual thickness, clear width, usable length after end checks, flatness, and defects on **each** board and leg blank. Mark which areas are intended for the top and which for the aprons. Check that milling can still yield the finished thickness and the top's cleanup allowance.

**Tools:** rule or tape, square, straightedge, pencil, the cutting layout, and the instructor's moisture meter if called for. Keep an offcut for setup and finish trials. **Stopping point:** measured and labeled stock, with the layout checked against it; do not cut a dependent part merely because the purchase label seems large enough.
