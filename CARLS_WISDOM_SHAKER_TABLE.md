Carl's central advice is to understand the parts and their relationships before buying or cutting wood. The plan is a starting point for a first project, and changes need to be carried through every affected part.

## Which plan are we building?

| Source | What Carl wants us to learn | How it applies here |
|---|---|---|

Carl says the full hall table is beyond the class's permitted size and complexity. Even its smaller lamp-table variation is a different design. Read its drawings for how they explain construction, rather than combining the two cut lists.

## A manageable first table

Carl explicitly encourages a beginner to omit the drawer and use four aprons to improve the chance of finishing in one term. A drawer adds fitting, clearance, runners, and extra joints. A shelf also adds work; it is not a free substitute.

That teaching advice supports our recorded **no-drawer** decision. Our proposed fourth front apron replaces the original two rails. It also changes the joints in the front legs, so Carl still needs to confirm the actual front-corner mortise layout and fit. General encouragement to simplify does not settle that detailed joint design.

Carl also recommends buying square leg stock instead of the article's more involved angled extraction from thick boards. We already have four reported **1 1/2 x 1 1/2 x 30 in** poplar blanks. The retained finished target, in **thickness x width x length** order, is **1 1/8 x 1 1/8 x 26 3/4 in**. The purchase dimensions remain unmeasured.

## The cut-list homework

Carl asks students to translate Schwarz's parts list into a Woodsmith-style materials list and cutting diagram. The exercise makes the lumber purchase understandable and forces the design choices to become explicit.

1. Name every required part and quantity. For us that means the top, four legs, four aprons, and two top cleats.
2. Write finished dimensions in **thickness x width x length** order. Keep tenons in the overall apron length.
3. Draw the actual boards, marking defects and the grain you want to show.
4. Fit clearly labeled rough blanks into those boards, allowing separately for kerfs, jointing, planing, trimming, and setup pieces.
5. Check the layout against the real stock before cutting.

Our [purchased-poplar cutting plan](CUTTING_DIAGRAM.md) is the working version of that homework. It uses the two approximately 60-in boards and four 30-in leg blanks already purchased.

**A numerical trap in the email:** its rough example says four 13-in aprons total 56 in. Four times 13 is **52 in**. Likewise, three 18 1/2-in top blanks total **55 1/2 in** before kerfs and end reserves. Treat the email's rounded totals as illustrative buying allowances, not an exact cutting layout. Our current plan labels those allowances separately.

## Joinery and the top

Carl describes several possible joint systems: traditional mortise and tenon, loose tenons, biscuits, dowels, and pocket screws. They change how a part's overall length relates to its visible shoulder span. A loose-tenon apron, for example, does not need an integral tenon projecting from each end.

Our proposal retains integral mortise-and-tenon apron joints. The article's **12 1/2-in overall apron length includes two 3/8-in tenons**, leaving an **11 3/4-in shoulder span**. Do not shorten those blanks using the email's alternative-joint example unless the joint system is deliberately changed and recorded.

For the top, both source plans accommodate seasonal movement. Our retained solution uses two top cleats and elongated screw holes across the tabletop grain. The Woodsmith plan uses different top-mount pieces and oversized holes; its hardware sizes are not a replacement specification for ours. Read [why moisture matters](CARLS_WISDOM_WOOD.md#storage-and-seasonal-movement) before attaching the top.

## What to confirm in class

- The two adjacent mortises in each front leg for the proposed fourth apron, including tenon fit and the 3/16-in apron setback.
- Whether the measured flat stock can finish at 3/4 in after flattening and panel cleanup.
- The taper method and workholding; see [Tapering the Legs](CARLS_WISDOM_TAPERING.md).
- The fitted top-cleat notches, movement holes, screw penetration, and the top bevel method.
- A finish tested on our purchased poplar. The article's cherry oil/lacquer schedule is historical context, not our selected finish.

**Stopping point:** labeled, measured stock and a checked layout preserve choices. Before any glue-up, complete the full dry fit and check square, twist, apron setbacks, and top fit. Record actual measurements in the [project guide](PROJECT_BASE.md#measurement-record).
